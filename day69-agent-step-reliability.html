🔗 Agent step reliability — a 96.5% agent gets two steps before it drops under 90% end to end, and twelve steps land it at 65.2%. But compounding is the boring half. The half nobody prices is which kind of failure you get: an abort you can escalate, or a confident answer built on a corrupted step. SELF-TEST

Your agent's reliability is a property of your tool layer.

Start with the arithmetic everyone can do and almost nobody does. An agent that picks the right tool with the right arguments 96.5% of the time — which would be a strong number on any single-call benchmark — completes a twelve-step trajectory 65.2% of the time, because 0.96512 is 0.652. It gets two steps before end-to-end accuracy falls under 90%, and nineteen before it is worse than a coin flip. Buying a much better model does not rescue this: at 99% per step it is still 88.6% over twelve steps and a coin flip at sixty-eight.

So far, so familiar. The part that actually decides whether you can ship the thing is what happens after a step goes wrong, and that is not a property of the model at all.

Two errors that are not the same error

A wrong tool call has two completely different fates. Either it errors — 404, schema violation, permission denied, NOT_FOUND — in which case the agent can see it happened and try again, excluding the tool that just failed. Or it returns something plausible — an empty list, a default object, the right shape with the wrong contents — in which case the agent proceeds on corrupted state and has no idea. Call the second kind's share silent. It is not in your agent config, your model card, or your eval harness. It is a property of how the people who wrote your tools chose to signal absence.

A retry after a loud error is not a coin flip repeated — it is better than the first attempt, because the failed tool is now excluded. With eight tools at 96.5%, the second attempt succeeds 97.0% of the time and the third 97.5%. That is why one retry per step is worth 12.7 points of end-to-end success (64.6% → 77.3%) and the second retry is worth 0.1. Retry budgets past one are theatre.

And it is why escalating a retry to the expensive model is nearly worthless here: it buys 0.3 points for 1.37 units of extra spend per task. The information that makes a retry work is in the error message, not in the model.

Downstream calls are an accidental verifier

Now the second tool-layer property. Once the state is corrupted, every later call is operating on garbage — and a call that validates its inputs will reject that garbage. A missing id, a mismatched schema, a total that does not reconcile. When that happens the agent sees an error, retries the step in front of it, cannot fix the step behind it, and gives up. It fails, but it fails loudly. Call that per-step probability trip.

Which gives the closed form the whole page turns on. With no retries and no verification:

success = p^H
wrong   = SUM over i of  p^i · (1−p) · silent · (1−trip)^(H−1−i)

Read the second line. A silent error at step i has H−1−i later calls to be caught by, so its chance of surviving to the user decays geometrically in how far from the end it happened. At trip = 0.22 over twelve steps, corrupting step 1 survives 6.5% of the time and corrupting step 12 survives always. The last step is the only one with nothing behind it.

A longer trajectory is more self-checking in its middle and equally blind at its end. Which is why the wrong answers you ship are not spread evenly across the plan: the last three of twelve steps are 25% of the steps and 52.7% of the confidently wrong answers.

The two dials do completely different jobs, and the simulation separates them cleanly. silent moves how often the agent is right — 99.98% success at silent=0 down to 64.6% at silent=1, because a loud error gets retried and a silent one cannot be. trip moves success by exactly nothing — 77.5% at every value from 0 to 0.5 — and moves only whether a failure is visible: confidently wrong falls 22.5% → 3.3% while known failure rises 0% → 19.2%. Input validation does not make your agent smarter. It makes your agent's mistakes arrive as exceptions, which is the only form in which you can do anything about them.

Put both dials at the two ends of what a real tool layer plausibly does and the same agent becomes two different products. Strict — typed errors, validated inputs (silent 0.20, trip 0.35): 91.5% success, 18 confidently wrong answers per thousand tasks. Permissive — empty results for missing data, no input validation (silent 0.85, trip 0.05): 68.6% success, 240 per thousand. Identical model, identical prompt, identical retry policy, and the bills are 12.18 against 11.92. A 13.6× difference in the thing that reaches a customer, bought entirely in the tool layer.

The recommendation I set out to make, and what happened to it

The obvious move is to add a verifier: a cheap model that reads a step's output and says whether it looks right. And the obvious way to spend it is uniformly — check every step, or sample every third one if you cannot afford all of them. That is the advice this page was going to give.

It is dominated. At an identical number of checks, placement moves the result more than the budget does:

One check on the last step costs 12.32 and leaves 1.7% confidently wrong. Three checks sampled across the trajectory cost 12.94 and leave 2.6% — more money, worse answers. Two at the tail cost 12.62 and leave 0.7%, which is exactly what checking all twelve steps achieves at 17.05. And three checks at the front cost 13.29 to take confidently wrong from 7.7% to 7.6% — a 1.43-unit-per-task spend that is indistinguishable from having bought nothing.

The mechanism is the geometric term again. A check at step j is worth (1−trip)H−1−j relative to a check at the end, because everything after j is already checking it for free. Over twelve steps at trip = 0.22 that is a 15.4× spread between the first position and the last. Uniform verification pays the same price at both.

And the second thing that lost: verification has two jobs

Having found that the tail is where the checks belong, the tempting conclusion is that checking the middle is waste. It is not — it is waste for one of the two objectives, and the page had to be rewritten to say so.

A verifier does exactly one mechanical thing: it converts a silent error into a loud one, so the retry machinery can fire. That has two entirely separate effects. It stops a corrupted answer reaching the user, which only matters near the end where nothing else would have caught it. And it repairs the step, which matters everywhere, because a repaired step is a step that still completes. So:

Two checks at the tail and twelve checks everywhere both reach 0.7% confidently wrong — but success is 80.0% against 93.9%. The extra ten checks buy nothing at all on the metric they are usually sold on, and 13.9 points on a different one, for 35% more compute. If you are trying not to ship a wrong answer, spend at the end. If you are trying to finish the task, you have to spend everywhere. These are not the same purchase and a single “accuracy” number hides which one you made.

Which also settles the model-tier question in an unexpected direction. Running every step on a model that is 4× the price and errs at 1% instead of 3.5% costs 47.86 per task for 93.0% success and 2.7% confidently wrong. The cheap model with a check on every step costs 17.05 for 93.9% and 0.7%. The expensive model is beaten on all three axes at 2.8× the price, and it would have to be priced at 1.43× the cheap one merely to tie on cost. Cutting per-step error by 3.5× is worth less than converting the errors you already have from silent to loud, because compounding punishes the error you cannot see far harder than the one you can.

The false positives are the real bill

A verifier that flags a correct step is not neutral. The agent does what you told it to do: it distrusts the tool it just used and picks again — off a distribution that was already pointing at the right answer. In this model a flag down-weights the tool the agent chose, so a false positive turns a step that was right into a step that is only 91.7% likely to be right, and can burn the retry budget outright.

Checking all twelve steps, as the false-positive rate goes 0% → 15%: success 95.1% → 83.9%, with 1.92 flags fired on correct steps per task and 5.1% of runs killed outright by one. The same verifier used only on the last four steps loses 3.4 points over the same range instead of 11.2. A verifier's precision is priced per check, so it is one more reason the budget wants to be small and well aimed.

The planner picks H, and it is a real dial

One parameter in all of this is chosen by a model at runtime and appears in no config file at all: how many steps the plan has. Cut the same task into more, smaller steps and each step gets easier — but there is an irreducible per-call selection error that does not shrink with the step. Model per-step error as work/H + perCall and the picture is not what the folklore says: going from 2 steps to 30, success barely moves (82.7% → 76.4%, and the decline is entirely the per-call term) while confidently wrong falls 14.9% → 2.1%. Finer decomposition does not make the agent more accurate. It converts wrong answers into known failures, because it adds downstream calls to trip over the corruption — and it costs a linear amount of compute to do it.

What to do on a real system

  • Instrument the silent share before you touch the model. Take a sample of wrong trajectories and ask, per step, whether a wrong call produced an exception or a plausible value. That single fraction moves shipped wrong answers by more than a model upgrade will.
  • Make your tools rude. Return NOT_FOUND, not an empty list. Return a typed error, not a default object. Validate inputs on every call even when the caller is your own agent — that validation is the cheapest verifier you will ever deploy and it runs on the path anyway.
  • Spend the verification budget at the end of the trajectory, unless you are optimising completion rather than correctness, in which case spend it everywhere and know that is what you bought.
  • One retry per step, and let the error message do the work. Excluding the tool that just failed is most of the value; escalating the retry to a bigger model is nearly none of it.
  • Price your verifier's false positives per check, not per task. A 4% FPR on twelve steps fires half a flag per task on work that was already correct.
  • Report three outcomes, never two. Success / known failure / confidently wrong. A dashboard that shows only a success rate cannot distinguish the two configurations in the red box above, and they are not the same product.
  • Count the steps in your plans. If per-step accuracy is 96.5% and your planner is emitting twenty-step trajectories, no amount of prompt work will fix the arithmetic.

🔨 How it is built

1. Nothing here simulates language

// a step is a CHOICE AMONG K TOOLS, not a piece of text:
//   correct mass  p
//   wrong mass    (1-p), spread evenly over the K-1 wrong tools
// and a wrong choice has a FATE, drawn separately:
//   loud   -> the agent sees an error and may retry, EXCLUDING the tool that failed
//   silent -> a plausible result comes back and the agent proceeds on corrupted state

There is no text, no model and no classifier anywhere in the engine. What is simulated is the only structure the question depends on: that tool calls are selections, that errors have two fates, that a retry knows something the first attempt did not, and that later calls may reject state the earlier ones broke.

2. A retry is arithmetic, not optimism

correctProb(p, K, triedWrong, dw) =
  mc = p·dw                                  // dw = (1-trust)^(number of flags so far)
  mw = ((1-p)/(K-1)) · (K-1-triedWrong)      // wrong tools not yet excluded
  mc / (mc + mw)

A loud error excludes a tool, so the retry's success probability genuinely rises: 96.5% → 97.0% → 97.5% at K=8. A verifier flag only down-weights, because the agent has no way to know the flag was right — which is exactly why a false positive is expensive and not merely wasteful.

3. Equal cost is enforced in check COUNT, not approximated

k = round(v·H)                       // the budget
front   -> j < k
tail    -> j >= H - k
sampled -> the midpoints of k equal blocks: round((i+0.5)·H/k - 0.5)

All three placements spend exactly k verifier calls, so the comparison between them needs no matching step at all. The midpoint form matters: a naive every H/k-th step stride puts its single check on the last step, which quietly hands the sampled strategy the tail strategy's answer and makes the whole result disappear.

4. Two closed forms, checked against the simulation

success = p^H
wrong   = SUM_i  p^i · (1-p) · silent · (1-trip)^(H-1-i)
value of a check at step j, relative to the last = (1-trip)^(H-1-j)

Derived by hand and then tested rather than trusted: against a 20,000-task world the simulation with retries disabled matches both lines to under half a point on every one of the parameter combinations in the test file. The per-step term is also the chart on the MEASURE tab, plotted against the measured distribution of where corruption actually happened.

5. One structural invariant worth more than a test

assert(success is IDENTICAL for trip = 0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.22, 0.35, 0.5)

Not approximately identical — bit-identical, because trip is only consulted once the state is already corrupted and a corrupted run can never be a success. That makes the claim “input validation changes the failure mode and not the success rate” a property of the construction rather than a finding, and the assertion is there so a later edit that accidentally lets trip touch a clean step fails loudly.

6. Four degeneracies, declared

Day 66 in this series lost its headline to a model that collapsed to exactly 0 and 1 at an extreme setting, so every corner is checked here and the ones that are genuinely degenerate are named on the page rather than quietly avoided:

K = 2      -> after ONE loud error the retry is correct with probability exactly 1.0
trust = 1  -> a false positive excludes the correct tool, so the redo is wrong with probability 1.0
silent = 0 -> confidently wrong is exactly 0, so verification has no channel at all and
              checking every step COSTS 0.9 points of success for 4.8 units of spend
recall=1, fpr=0, check every step -> wrong exactly 0.0%, success 99.98%; not a verifier, an oracle

The page defaults sit away from all four (K=8, trust 0.6, silent 0.55, recall 0.80, fpr 0.04) and every headline number is reported with all three outcomes strictly between 0 and 1. One more that is not degeneracy but is worth not over-claiming: the retry slider is flat past 2 — 77.5% at 2, 3 and 4 retries, identical to three decimal places — so the page says one retry and stops.

7. What the tests pin

// the tool layer, not the model, decides what reaches a user
assert(wrong(permissive) > 8 × wrong(strict))            // measured 13.6x over 5 worlds
assert(success is bit-identical across the whole trip sweep)
assert(success strictly decreasing in the silent share)

// the closed forms ARE the simulation
assert(|sim - p^H| < 0.005)                    on a 20,000-task world
assert(|sim wrong - closed form| < 0.005)      over 8 parameter combinations

// the null results, kept because they are true
assert(wrong(1 check at the tail) < wrong(3 checks sampled)); // and it costs LESS
assert(wrong(3 checks at the front) > wrong(no checks) - 0.005);
assert(wrong(2 at the tail) <= wrong(all 12) + 0.003);
assert(success(all 12) > success(2 at the tail) + 0.10);     // the OTHER job
assert(cost(strong tier) > 2.5 × cost(cheap + verify all)
       && success(strong) < success(cheap + verify all));
assert(success(escalate retries) - success(plain) < 0.01);

// and nothing is collapsed or out of range
assert(every one of 3,240 slider configurations returns three rates that sum to 1);
assert(K=2 retry probability === 1  &&  default K=8 retry probability < 0.98);

The third block is the one worth having. It says that the thing this page was going to recommend — spread your verification budget over the trajectory — is beaten at equal cost by one check in the right place, and that the strategy which does win on the headline metric wins nothing on the other one. Both halves sit in the test file so the inconvenient one cannot be quietly deleted later.
