An index is a cache, and nobody wrote its invalidation protocol.

Every other cache in your stack has one. HTTP has ETag and max-age; your CDN has a purge API; your ORM's second-level cache has a write hook. A retrieval index has a cron job and a hope. It is a copy of a corpus that keeps changing, and the entire question of how wrong it is comes down to two numbers per document: how often that document's answers change, λ, and how often you look at it, r.

Those two give one closed form, and it is not the one people guess. A document scanned every 1/r hours whose answers change as a Poisson process of rate μ is materially stale, averaged over the cycle, with probability

S(x) = 1 - (1 - e^-x) / x ,   x = μ/r

Not "half the refresh period" — that is the answer to a different question. S is the average of a saturating exponential over the period: at x = 1 it is 0.368, at x = 2 it is 0.568, and it approaches 1 from below rather than crossing it. Small x gives x/2, which is where the folklore comes from, and the folklore is only right when you are already refreshing far faster than the document changes.

The dashboard is measuring the wrong average

Now sum that over a corpus, and notice that there are two completely different ways to average it. Your indexing dashboard averages per document: 300 documents, count how many hold a stale copy, report a percentage. Your users average per query, and queries are not uniform over documents — they are Zipf. At the page defaults the two numbers are 1.21% and 4.87%: a 4× gap, from nothing but the weighting.

The multiplier between them has a name and a one-line SQL query. It is the inverse participation ratio of your query distribution, D · Σ w², where w is each document's share of queries. At Zipf 1.0 over 300 documents it is 12.5. At Zipf 0.6 it is 2.4 and at Zipf 1.4 it is 46.0 — and across that range the stale-answer rate goes 12.8 → 79.3 per thousand while the document-weighted number on the dashboard falls, 1.35% → 1.03%. Skew makes your index look fresher and your product worse, at the same time, for the same money.

And the second averaging trap is the one worth the whole page. Hold the corpus fixed, hold the total edits per hour fixed, hold the refresh budget and the bill fixed, and change only which documents get edited. When edits are independent of popularity the same system ships 9.1 stale answers per thousand queries. When edits follow popularity it ships 57.3 — a 6.3× difference. Meanwhile the document-weighted freshness the dashboard reports goes from 1.25% stale to 0.92%. It improves. The number you watch moves the wrong way while the number you sell gets six times worse, and the correlation that caused it appears in no indexing config, no vector-store setting and no eval harness.

It is not mysterious once stated: popular documents are popular because they are about live things — prices, availability, policy, status — and live things are what people edit. The corpus property is real, it is measurable from two columns of your own logs, and nothing in the stack asks you for it.

A stale chunk has two fates, and only one is visible

When a document's answer changes, the index still holds the old text. What happens next depends on something no freshness metric records: how big the edit was. A small edit — a number, a date, a clause — leaves the chunk's embedding essentially where it was, so the stale chunk is still retrieved for the same query and the model answers confidently from it. A large edit moves the chunk, the query no longer matches it, and it drops out of the top k — so you get a miss: an abstention, a worse-but-honest answer from somewhere else, a support ticket instead of a wrong number.

With one copy of every fact this is a pure mode dial and the simulation proves it: as the small-edit share goes 0 → 1, the correct-answer rate is bit-identical at 95.13% while stale answers go 0 → 48.7 per thousand and misses go 4.87% → 0. Editing style does not change how often you are right. It changes whether being wrong looks like an error or like an answer.

Turn duplication on and the invariant breaks by exactly the amount it should: 0.45 points across the same sweep, because a retrievability change is what lets a fresh duplicate outrank a stale original. That is the whole mechanism of the last third of this page.

The policy ranking inverts, so the argument was never about the policy

Give the refresh budget a shape. Uniform cadence, proportional to change rate, proportional to query volume, or proportional to √(w · λ). All four spend exactly the same money, bisected onto the same bill, so the comparison is legal. Now read the table on the MEASURE tab twice.

When edits are independent of popularity, proportional-to-change-rate is the worst policy on offer — 12.7 per thousand against 9.1 for a flat cadence and 3.8 for the square-root rule. When edits follow popularity it is the best, 16.7 against 57.3 for the flat cadence that just beat it. Same budget, same bill, same code: the ordering of your options is decided by a corpus property, not by the policy.

The first half of that is not new — it is Cho and Garcia-Molina's crawler result from 2000, that a uniform refresh policy beats one proportional to change rate, reproduced here on a retrieval index rather than a web crawl. Proportional-to-change pours money into documents that will be stale again before you finish, and the money comes out of documents that would have stayed fresh. What is new is the inversion: correlate the edits with the queries and proportional-to-change stops being a waste and becomes, accidentally, proportional-to-queries. At perfect correlation the three shapes are algebraically the same vector — the simulation checks it, they agree to 3×10-16 — so the whole policy debate collapses into a single row.

The recommendation this page set out to make

If the allocation is what matters, solve it. Minimise Σ wd S(μd/rd) subject to a budget, per document, with a Lagrange multiplier and four options: abandon the document, sweep it at some rate, wire an invalidation event to it, or take it out of the index altogether and fetch it live at query time. Water-fill the price until the bill lands on the budget. This is a textbook problem, it takes forty lines, and it produces a genuinely interesting artefact — a mode map over the (popularity, volatility) plane, with a live-fetch region, a sweep region, and an abandonment region whose boundaries are prices rather than opinions.

It loses.

At an identical bill the optimum ships 17.7 stale answers per thousand and the one-line square-root rule ships 10.5 — 69% worse — while winning on the objective it was given, 8.0 against 9.9 per thousand of predicted stale copies. Its 99th-percentile error is 199.6 hours old against 5.6. And the tell is in the mode map: it put 201 of 300 documents on webhook-only, because per document a webhook is cheap and clean, and 66.2% of the stale answers it ships come from duplicated facts.

The objective is separable per document. The failure is not. A fact with a copy in a second document is a coupling between two documents, and no per-document value function can represent it — so the optimiser buys the mode whose weakness its own arithmetic cannot see. This is not a bug in the solver. It is the solver being exactly as good as its objective.

And the damage is itself correlation-dependent, which is what would make this hard to catch in production. At ρ = 0 the optimum all but ties the heuristic — 3.9 against 3.8 — because at that end the documents it puts on a webhook are documents almost nobody queries. Move the edits onto the popular ones and the same solver, same objective, same budget, goes from a tie to 69% worse. A solver that behaves on your staging corpus is not evidence about your production one.

And then the correction lost as well

The obvious repair: never leave a document on events alone, always put a floor sweep underneath. Re-run the same water-fill with "webhook + sweep" as the only event-bearing option. It predicts the best number on the page — 5.8 per thousand of stale copies, better than every heuristic — and ships 32.7, worse than the naive optimum it was supposed to fix and three times the heuristic it was supposed to beat. The floor sweep is bought out of the same budget, so it starves the sweeps that were doing the work.

What actually works is deleting the option. The same water-fill, restricted to sweep rates, ships 8.3 per thousand — the best number on the page — and √(w·λ), which is two columns of SQL and no solver at all, ships 10.5. Across the whole correlation range those two stay within 2.2 stale answers per thousand of each other and trade places twice (3.8 vs 4.7 at ρ = 0, 10.5 vs 8.3 at 0.55, 16.7 vs 16.7 at 1.0) — so the rule of thumb is not merely close to the constrained optimum, it sometimes ships fewer wrong answers than it. The freedom was the bug: every extra mode the optimiser could choose was a mode whose failure its objective could not price.

The webhook has a floor, and something worse under it

Event-driven invalidation is the answer everyone reaches for, and it has a hard floor that no budget removes. If an edit's event never fires, the copy stays stale until some later edit of the same document happens to fire one. Walking backwards through the edit stream, the copy is stale exactly when the most recent answer-changing edit was not followed by any detected edit, which is

floor = m(1-detect) / (detect + m(1-detect))

— 1.37% of answers at a 97% detection rate, and note what is missing from that expression: the edit rate. The floor is a property of your webhook, not of your corpus. At 90% detection it is 4.76%, and no amount of reindexing money touches it, because there is no reindexing in this policy to spend money on.

Then set the webhook to perfect. Every edit detected, zero lag, instant re-embedding. It still ships 35.5 stale answers per thousand. The event fires for the document that changed, and the stale copy is sitting in a document that did not — a duplicated paragraph, a summary table, an FAQ entry, a quickstart that repeats the reference. Nothing will ever invalidate it except an edit of its own. Perfect invalidation is perfect only over the set of documents your writers touched, and that set is not the set of chunks that contain the fact.

At equal spend the picture is Pareto-messy in a way worth knowing about. The webhook ships 54.5 per thousand with a 99th-percentile error age of 191.7 hours; a flat sweep at the same 5.82 units an hour ships 80.3 with a p99 of 6.5 hours. One is better on the mean and eight days worse on the tail. The square-root sweep, at that same price, ships 33.8 with a p99 of 27.7 and dominates the webhook on both axes — which is not an argument against events so much as an argument that "we invalidate on write" is not by itself a freshness strategy.

The cheapest fix on the page is in the reranker

When one copy of a fact is current and the other is stale, both are retrievable, and the retriever has no idea which chunk is newer. It ranks on similarity. So the answer is decided by a coin flip that your indexing budget cannot influence — unless you break the tie on the chunk's own timestamp.

Preferring the newer chunk when two retrieved chunks cover the same fact takes the webhook from 54.5 stale answers per thousand to 20.7, and takes the perfect webhook to 0.0. It takes the square-root sweep from 10.5 to 6.9. For comparison, moving the sweep budget from 6.0 to 8.0 units an hour — a 33% larger indexing bill — buys 19 per thousand. A tiebreak on a timestamp you already store buys 33.8, and it is the only lever on this page that costs nothing per hour.

What you are actually buying

One last piece of arithmetic that reframes the budget argument. A scan does not have to re-embed: hash the source, compare, and pay for the model only when the content moved. So of the default 8.00 units an hour, 5.63 are embeddings and 2.37 are looking. The corpus generates 6.0 edits an hour, so 94% of the embedding bill is fixed by your writers and not by your policy; a sweep only coalesces bursts, which at these rates saves 6%. You are not buying freshness with a bigger budget. You are buying news — how soon you find out — and that is the cheap 30% of the bill.

Which sets a ceiling on the whole discussion. Skipping the index and fetching every document live at query time costs 75.0 units an hour here and ships zero stale answers, so no refresh policy can ever be worth more than that, and the default 8.00 is buying 11% of the ceiling. Turn the budget up to 200 and the optimiser moves all 300 documents to live fetch and stops indexing entirely — the model's declared saturation point, and also the correct engineering answer for a document whose answers change hourly and whose queries are rare.

What to do on a real system

  • Compute D · Σw² from a week of query logs before you touch the cadence. That single number is the multiplier between your dashboard and your users, and it is 12.5 on a normal Zipf query mix.
  • Correlate your edit log against your query log. If the documents people ask about are the documents your writers keep editing, your stale-answer rate is six times what a document-weighted freshness metric implies, and no cadence change fixes the measurement.
  • Weight the refresh by √(queries × change). Two columns of SQL, within one stale answer per thousand of a numeric optimum, and it beats every uniform, every ∝change and every ∝query allocation at the same bill.
  • Do not let an optimiser put a document on webhook-only. The objective is separable and duplicated content is not, so that is precisely the mode it will over-buy.
  • Add a recency tiebreak to the reranker before you add budget. It is the only fix here that costs nothing per hour, and it is worth more than perfect invalidation.
  • Report stale answers per thousand and the p99 error age, never index freshness. A webhook and a sweep can tie on the mean and differ by eight days on the tail.
  • Decide which documents should not be in the index at all. High volatility with low query volume is a live-fetch document, and the boundary is a price you can compute, not a taste.

🔨 How it is built

1. Nothing here simulates language, embeddings or a model

// a query is: a DOCUMENT drawn from a Zipf popularity law, at a TIME.
// the index holds that document as of its last refresh. Three questions, in order:
//   1. has an answer-changing edit happened since that refresh?          -> Poisson, rate m*lambda
//   2. is the stale chunk still RETRIEVABLE for this query?              -> small edit vs large
//   3. does the fact have a second copy in some other document?          -> and is THAT copy stale?

There is no text, no vector and no similarity score anywhere in the engine. What is simulated is the only structure the question depends on: that an index is a stale copy, that staleness is a race between an edit process and a scan process, that a chunk can be wrong and still be the best lexical match, and that the same fact can sit in two documents with two different refresh histories.

2. The backward construction, so a world survives every slider

A = -ln(u) / (m * lambda_d)      // age of the last answer-changing edit, exactly Exp(m*lambda)
e = t - A                        // when the index went wrong
if (e <= 0) correct              // nothing material since the index was built at t = 0

Rather than generate a per-document edit history over 720 hours — which at the hot end would be thousands of arrivals and would be re-generated every time a slider moved — each query samples the backward age of the last relevant edit. For a Poisson process that is exact and stationary, it costs one uniform, and it makes every configuration read the same pre-drawn numbers. The forward stream is generated in exactly one place: the single-document timeline on the MEASURE tab, which is display-only and labelled as such.

3. The three exact probabilities the engine needs

stale copy, scanned at rate r:   S(m*lambda/r),  S(x) = 1 - (1-e^-x)/x
embeddings per hour at rate r:   r * (1 - e^-(lambda/r))     // a hash check, not a re-embed
webhook floor:                   m(1-detect) / (detect + m(1-detect))

All three are derived rather than fitted, and all three are checked against the simulation rather than trusted. The third is the one worth deriving out loud: walk backwards through the edit stream, and each arrival is independently detected (stop, the copy is current) or material and undetected (stop, the copy is stale) or neither (keep walking). The ratio of those two stopping probabilities is the floor, and lambda cancels — a webhook's floor is a property of the webhook.

4. Equal cost is bisected, not estimated

scaleToBudget(shape) -> geometric bisection on a scalar until cost(shape * g) == budget
optimalAlloc(budget) -> geometric bisection on the Lagrange price mu until the bill fits,
                        then spendRest() scales the whole rate vector until it is EXACT

Every comparison on the page is vertical. A shape (uniform, ∝change, ∝query, √(wλ)) is a normalised vector that gets scaled until its bill equals the target, and the water-fill picks per-document modes at a price that is bisected to the same target. A Lagrangian relaxation over a discrete option set lands under the budget, which would quietly hand the optimum a discount, so the leftover is spent by scaling the whole rate vector — at a high budget that buys nothing measurable, which is the flat tail on the frontier chart. The tables print the achieved cost in their own column so that a claim of equal cost can be audited by reading the page rather than by trusting it.

5. Cost has two currencies, because that is the real asymmetry

sweep   : 0.02 per look  + 1.00 per look that finds a change
webhook : 1.00 per EDIT that fires an event   (including the cosmetic ones)
live    : 0.15 per QUERY   (no index, no staleness)

This is what makes the event-versus-cadence question a real trade rather than a slogan. A webhook pays per edit and cannot coalesce a burst; a sweep pays per look and coalesces everything inside a period; a live fetch is priced in queries and so gets cheaper relative to the alternatives exactly on the documents that change fastest. All three land on the same frontier axis, which is the only reason the mode map has boundaries.

6. Degeneracies, declared

Day 66 in this series lost a headline to a model that collapsed at an extreme setting, so every corner here is checked and the genuinely degenerate ones are named on the page instead of avoided:

material = 0        -> zero stale answers; there is nothing to be stale about
small = 0           -> zero stale answers, all misses; the wrong-answer channel is shut
small = 1, dup = 0  -> zero misses; every stale copy is retrieved and answered from
budget = 200        -> the optimiser puts all 300 documents on live fetch and stops indexing
rho = 1             -> the change, query and sqrt shapes become the SAME vector (3e-16 apart)
detect = 1, lag = 0 -> a perfect webhook. NOT degenerate: 35.5 stale answers per thousand,
                       and 0.0 only once a recency tiebreak is added on top

The last line is the page's best result and the reason the corner was worth checking at all. The defaults sit away from all of them: 45% material, 65% small, 30% duplicated, 97% detected, 0.5 h lag, 8.00 units an hour against a 75.0 ceiling, and every headline is reported with all three outcomes strictly between 0 and 1.

7. What the tests pin

assert(webhook floor is INDEPENDENT of the edit rate)                   // 1x to 24x

// the parameter in nobody's config
assert(stale(rho=1) > 5 x stale(rho=0)  at an identical bill)

// the mode dial, and where it stops being one

assert(stale(prop-to-change) < stale(uniform)/3 at rho = 1)   // the SAME policy, inverted

assert(stale(optimalPlus) > stale(optimal)  &&  predicted(optimalPlus) is the lowest of all)

assert(|stale(sqrt) - stale(sweepOnly)| < 0.002 at every rho)   // the rule of thumb tracks it

The third and fourth blocks are the ones worth having. They say that the thing this page set out to recommend — solve the allocation properly — is beaten at equal cost by two columns of SQL, that the obvious repair is beaten by both, and that the optimiser wins the whole time on the objective it was handed. All three sit in the test file so the inconvenient ones cannot be quietly deleted later.

🎉 AIFromZero Day 70 shipped.

A RAG system does not go stale because the cron job is too slow. It goes stale because the documents people ask about are the documents people edit, because a small edit leaves a wrong chunk perfectly retrievable, and because the webhook fires for the document that changed rather than for the one holding the copy.
