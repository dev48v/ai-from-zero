SELF-TEST

scroll here to run…

A preference judgement is not one measurement. It is a collision between a customer, a request and a person, and only one of those three is in the spreadsheet twice.

The eval looks simple. Ship the feature to half the traffic, take four thousand requests, show a human the control's answer and the feature's answer side by side, and ask which is better. Count the wins. You have four thousand numbers, each of them 0, ½ or 1, and the average is 0.5 plus the lift. Every A/B testing tutorial ever written now tells you the standard error of a mean: the sample standard deviation over the square root of four thousand. Here that is 0.0073, so a lift of a percentage point and a half is significant, and you ship.

Run that with the feature turned off — the same model on both sides, a genuine A/A test where every rating is a coin flip about nothing — and it ships 65.6% of the time. The estimate's real standard deviation across independent repeats of the whole experiment is 0.0307, four times the number the formula reported. The formula is not approximately right. It is wrong by a factor.

The reason is that the four thousand rows are not four thousand facts. A customer whose traffic happens to suit the feature contributes a run of requests that all lean the same way. A rater who happens to like the feature's house style contributes a run of ratings that all lean the same way. Those two runs cross: rater 11 judges requests from hundreds of customers, and customer 3's requests are judged by most of the panel. There is no way to slice the file into independent blocks, because the two groupings are not nested inside one another. That is the whole subject of this page.

The design effect, and the number that is in nobody's config

The classical correction is one line. If observations come in clusters, the variance of the mean is inflated by 1 + (mᵉᵗᵗ − 1)ρ, where ρ is the intraclass correlation and mᵉᵗᵗ is the variance-weighted cluster size — Σn² / Σn, not the average. That distinction is the entire story. The customer intraclass correlation here is 0.1336 and the mean requests per customer is 8. Set the traffic to a Zipf law, which is what real traffic is, and mᵉᵗᵗ becomes 112.9, fourteen times larger, without one customer's mean changing. Nothing in the experiment's configuration mentions it.

The rater panel obeys the same arithmetic and nobody applies it there. Twenty-four raters splitting four thousand ratings gives mᵉᵗᵗ = 166.7, and even at a rater intraclass correlation of 0.0143 — a number that looks like zero — the design effect is 3.38. The formula predicts the rater-clustered standard error to 0.0133 against a measured 0.0135. A correlation of one and a half percent, multiplied by a hundred and sixty-six, is not small.

Why picking one axis does not work

Cluster by customer and the false-positive rate falls from 65.6% to 12.0%. Cluster by rater and it falls to 39.4%. Neither reaches 5%, because each estimator is correcting one source of dependence while treating the other as noise. The two-way estimator of Cameron, Gelbach and Miller adds the two one-way variances and subtracts the variance clustered on the intersection, which corrects the double count; it reaches 7.2%. What is left is not a second kind of dependence. It is that five hundred customers, one of whom supplies an eighth of the sample, are worth 35.4 clusters, and a cluster-robust variance estimate with thirty-five effective clusters is biased downward.

The published repair, and what it is deaf to

The standard answer to too-few-clusters is the wild cluster bootstrap-t: impose the null, flip the sign of every residual in a cluster together, do that a couple of hundred times, and read the critical value off the resulting distribution instead of a normal table. It works. On Zipf traffic with the rater panel switched off it takes 6.6% down to 2.4%, and you can watch it discover the problem — the critical value it hands back is 2.30 where the normal table says 1.96. Switch the traffic to flat and the rater panel on, and it takes 16.8% to 15.6% with a critical value of 2.02. It repaired nothing, and it reported that everything was fine, because the axis it was pointed at genuinely was fine. A method that counts clusters cannot tell you that you chose the wrong thing to count.

The obvious extension — draw a sign for each customer, a sign for each rater, multiply them — looks like it should give a two-way bootstrap, and it is provably the wrong quantity. Two observations get correlated bootstrap weights only when the product of their four signs has a non-zero expectation, and that happens only when they share both labels. So the resampling variance of the numerator is the sum of squared cell sums, which is the term the two-way formula subtracts. On the default configuration that is 11.92× smaller than the customer-level variance it was supposed to reproduce, and the page checks the identity numerically rather than asserting it.

The thing that actually works is not a formula

Every repair above is an argument about how to analyse a file that has already been collected. The number that moves the result most is which rows are in the file. Cap how much of the eval any one customer may contribute — sample eight hundred requests from the head instead of taking whatever traffic offered — and at an identical four thousand ratings and an identical bill the estimate's true standard deviation falls from 0.0307 to 0.0175, which is 3.08× the variance — three times the ratings you did not buy. Power against the same real effect goes from 39.4% to 58.0%. Sample every customer equally and it is 4.51× and 79.8%.

Two honest caveats, both declared rather than hidden. First: in this model a customer's tilt toward the feature is drawn independently of how much traffic it sends, so re-weighting customers does not move the population quantity being estimated — the truth stays 4.13pp at every cap. In a real system it would move, and the cap silently changes the question from "averaged over traffic" toward "averaged over customers". Say which one you meant. Second: capping makes clustering by customer worse, 12.0% to 17.2%, because once the traffic is flat the customer axis is no longer the dominant one and the rater axis you were ignoring is all that is left.

Five things that follow, and four of them are backwards

More raters per request costs power. At a fixed rating budget, k raters per request buys R/k requests. The sampling variance of the mean is (k·between + within) / R, which rises with k for any positive between-request variance, so the item scores get more reliable — 0.512 to 0.823 — and the experiment gets less sensitive, power 49.7% to 31.1%. Three raters and a majority vote, the most common recommendation in the field, is the worst cell on the table: a majority vote throws away the margin, and its standard error is 0.0402 against 0.0306 for the same three ratings averaged. Extra raters pay only when producing the A/B pair costs money of its own, and the optimum is a square root: here you would need generation to cost 2.65× a rating before k = 3 wins.

The 1/p² dilution rule does not apply. Everyone knows that if the feature only changes p of requests, the diluted effect is pδ and you need 1/p² the sample. That derivation assumes the untouched requests still carry variance. In a preference comparison they do not: if the feature did not fire, the two answers are the same text, and the rating is a forced tie with no noise in it at all. Dilution shrinks the effect and the variance in the same proportion, so at p = 0.15 the ratings needed rise 6.76× under the naive standard error — that is 1/p, not 1/p² — and 1.36× once the standard error is clustered, because at that point the variance is dominated by the customer effect, which dilution also mutes.

The standard fix for dilution is a collider. "Only analyse the requests where the feature actually fired" is right when the flag is a property of the request, decided before the experiment. It is catastrophic when the flag is logged from what the treatment arm did, because then the flagged set has been selected on the very difference you are about to measure. At κ = 1 a feature worth exactly zero reads +12.93pp on the flagged set with a 99.4% false-positive rate, and the trigger rate is held at 30% the whole way, so nothing in a dashboard would look wrong. The size of the bias is predicted to half a basis point by a two-dimensional Gaussian quadrature that runs no simulation at all.

Always-valid inference is valid against exactly one thing. Peeking is real: on genuinely independent data a fixed-n z-test costs 3.0% at one look and 14.0% at ten. The mSPRT of Johari, Pekelis and Walsh is the published cure — a mixture likelihood ratio you may read at every observation. Point it at this traffic with a naive standard error and it spends 43.0% at one look and 46.0% at ten, so almost none of its error is peeking at all; it was never the disease here. Feed it the two-way standard error and it is honest again, 2.0% at one look and 3.0% at ten — at 21.0% power, because an honest sequential test on a dependent sample of four thousand mostly does not stop.

Randomising the order is free; rating both orders is not. A rater who prefers whichever answer is shown second is a bias, not a variance: hold the control first and the A/A estimate reads +6.91pp on a true effect of zero and the test rejects 73.7% of the time. Flip a coin per rating and the bias is gone exactly — 0.35pp, inside the Monte-Carlo error — at no cost, because at a position bias of zero the coin-flip design is bit-identical to the fixed one. Rating every request twice, once in each order, removes the same bias and costs power — 43.7% against 46.1% — because it halves the requests it can buy.

And the cheap judge

A model judging the same pair costs a fortieth of a person, so at any budget you get forty times as many judgements. Variance falls like 1/n; the judge's systematic preference for the feature's style does not fall at all. The judge is estimating 8.48pp where the truth is 4.13pp, a bias of 4.35pp — bigger than the thing being measured — so its error bottoms out at 4.90pp and forty thousand more calls do not move it. It wins anyway below about $150, because at $60 you can afford fifty human judgements and fifty human judgements are worse than that. Prediction-powered inference, which spends part of the budget on human labels and uses the judge to rectify the rest, is the textbook answer to exactly this situation, and tuned over twenty (φ, λ) pairs it does not beat spending everything on humans at any of seven budgets on this page. The reason is the same as everywhere else: the variance the judge could cancel lives at the customer level, which the judge already shares, so there is little left for it to explain.

The human column tells the same story in the other direction. Going from $2,400 to $4,800 — two thousand more human judgements — moves the RMSE from 2.79pp to 2.75pp. You did not buy precision. You bought more requests from the same handful of customers.

What to actually do

  • Decide what a cluster is before you look at the file, and expect there to be two of them: whoever sent the request and whoever judged it.
  • Report Σn²/Σn for both axes next to the sample size. It is two lines of code and it is the number that decides the test.
  • Sample the eval set with a cap per customer. It is the only intervention here that makes the test both more valid and more sensitive, and it costs nothing.
  • One rating per request unless generating the pair is expensive. If you do collect several, average them; never take a majority vote.
  • Randomise presentation order per rating. Always. It is free.
  • Never define the analysis set from a flag the treatment arm wrote.
  • If you use a model judge, measure its bias against a human sample first; if the bias is the size of your effect, extra judge calls are worthless.

What is simulated, and what is not

No language. A request is a number saying how well it suits the feature, a customer is a number, a rater is a number, and a rating is a logistic comparison of the two answers with a tie band around zero. Everything is deterministic given a seed. The population preference — the quantity every design here is trying to estimate — is computed by Gaussian quadrature rather than by simulation, so "power" and "false-positive rate" are measured against a known truth instead of against another random number. Every rate on this page is a Monte-Carlo estimate and every table states its Monte-Carlo error; the self-test checks conservation laws, the closed forms, the declared corners, determinism, and the specific claims quoted in the headline. A separate Node verifier re-runs the extracted engine against independently written oracles — a different generator, forward simulators, exact enumeration over sign patterns, and quadrature by a different rule.

🔨 How it is built

1 · The traffic, which is the whole experiment

M requests are handed to U customers by a Zipf law and the largest-remainder method, so the total is exactly M and the mean requests per customer is exactly the configured number at every setting of the skew dial. Only the shape moves. Owners are then interleaved through the stream by a fixed shuffle, because a large customer sends steadily rather than in one block, and a page that let the big customer arrive first would be measuring an ordering artefact. The variance-weighted cluster size Σn²/Σn and Kish's effective cluster count (Σn)²/Σn² fall out of that array and decide almost everything else.

2 · A world, and a rating

A world draws one tilt per customer, one per request and one per rater, all normal. A rating picks a rater uniformly, adds the customer tilt, the request tilt, the rater tilt, the position term and a logistic noise draw, and returns 1, ½ or 0 according to whether the total clears the tie band. When the feature did not fire on a request the two answers are identical, so the rating is a forced tie: no noise, no position, no information and no variance. That last clause is why the dilution numbers on this page are not the ones everybody quotes, and it is a modelling choice worth disagreeing with out loud.

3 · One standard-error function

There is a single cluster-robust routine. Give every observation its own cluster and it is the naive standard error — that is not an approximation, it is an identity, and the self-test checks it to machine precision. Clustering by request is the same thing again whenever each request is rated once. So the five rows of the headline table are one function called with five different keys, which is the point being made: the argument is not about statistics, it is about which column of the same spreadsheet you decided to call independent.

The two-way estimator adds the customer and rater variances and subtracts the variance clustered on the customer×rater intersection. Those cells are sparse, so the ids are compacted — through a flat lookup while the grid is small and a Map when the panel gets big enough that a dense grid would be millions of slots. The result is floored at the larger of the two one-way estimates, because a variance estimate smaller than a valid variance estimate is not a repair.

4 · The truth, without simulating anything

Everything a rating depends on except the logistic noise is normal, so the population preference is a one-dimensional integral over the sum of the customer and rater tilts and the request tilt, evaluated by Simpson's rule on a wide grid. When the trigger flag has been selected on the outcome it becomes a two-dimensional integral with a tail-probability weight, which is why the collider table can print a prediction next to a measurement and have them agree to half a basis point. The normal CDF is West's double-precision rational approximation and the inverse is Acklam's with one Halley step against it, so probit(ncdf(x)) returns x.

5 · The bootstrap

The wild cluster bootstrap-t imposes the null, multiplies every residual in a cluster by a shared Rademacher sign, and recomputes the t statistic. Only the cluster sums and counts are needed, so a replication is O(number of clusters) rather than O(number of ratings), which is what makes two hundred replications per world affordable inside a page. The product-weight variant is included because it is the natural thing to try and it is wrong: the page computes its exact resampling variance and the sum of squared cell sums and shows they are the same number.

6 · Rendering

Every table on this page is a few hundred to a few thousand simulated experiments, so the modules compute lazily: an IntersectionObserver marks a card as wanted when it comes near the viewport and a scheduler runs one module per animation frame, so the page stays responsive while it fills in. Each module declares which configuration fields it actually reads, so dragging a slider only invalidates the cards that depend on it. The results are cached on that reduced key.

7 · Verification

The self-test at the bottom of the MEASURE tab runs in the browser on every render: identities (the naive standard error is the cluster-robust one at one observation per cluster; the mean cluster size is invariant to the skew dial; the cap never moves the truth), the closed forms against the simulator in the regime where they are valid, the declared corners (zero position bias makes the fixed and swapped designs bit-identical; zero rater variance makes the two-way estimator collapse onto the customer one), determinism, monotonicity of each sweep, and the specific numbers quoted in the headline. A separate Node verifier re-runs the same extracted engine against oracles written a different way — another generator, forward simulators that draw a rating one at a time, exact enumeration over all sign patterns for the bootstrap identity, and quadrature by a different rule.
