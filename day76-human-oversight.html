Is the turnover a corner case?

The criterion is re-optimised at every cell, so each entry is the error rate at which that reviewer, working to that cost ratio, stops helping more than the model is improving. Rows are reviewer sensitivity; columns are how many false alarms one missed error is declared to be worth.

The reviewer is not getting lazy. They are getting it right.

A reviewer who flags too readily buys catches with false alarms. The threshold that minimises their expected cost sits where the likelihood ratio equals the odds against an error divided by the price of a miss — so when errors get rarer, the threshold rises, and the catch rate falls out of it. The closed form is k = d′/2 + ln(β)/d′; the column beside it is the same minimum found by brute search over the whole criterion axis, which is how the page knows the algebra is right.

Catch rate and false-alarm rate are two readings of one dial, so given the sensitivity either determines the other with nothing free in between. That relation is the ROC curve, and the area under it has a closed form that this page checks by integrating the curve itself with Gauss–Legendre nodes generated by Newton iteration.

What it costs to hold the catch rate you had

Freeze the threshold where it sat when the model was bad and the catch rate stays where it was — that is what a threshold is. The bill arrives as false alarms, and the reviewer’s own expected cost goes up, which is exactly why they moved. The last column is the number nobody computes: the price a missed error would have to be declared to carry for the optimal threshold to land back on that catch rate.

More review is not more review

Send only the items the model is least sure about. Two things move at once and they separate cleanly: the human now sees a larger share of the errors, and the queue they see has a higher error rate, so their own threshold comes back down. Freezing the threshold at its unrouted value isolates the first effect from the second.

A second pair of eyes, looking at the same answer

Two reviewers who were both shown the model’s answer do not bring two independent readings. Part of what each of them sees is the same thing — the answer itself, and how plausible it looks — so their evidence correlates, and the correlation is the whole reason the second one is worth less than the first. Both thresholds are re-optimised for the rule they are used under, because a pair should not sit where a soloist sits.

The obvious repair is to hide the model’s answer from the second reviewer, which takes the correlation to zero and costs them whatever the answer was worth as evidence. That is a trade with a crossover, and the crossover is computable.

Planting errors on purpose

Put known-bad items into the review queue. The reviewer’s capacity does not grow, so each planted item displaces a real one and the load is identical to doing nothing — but the error rate they meet goes up, their threshold comes back down, and the catch rate on the real items rises with it. Airport X-ray screening has run this for decades under the name threat image projection. The two effects pull opposite ways, so there is an interior best rate.

It does not always pay. A reviewer good enough to be catching nearly everything is limited by nothing that a threshold can fix, and every planted item is then pure loss.

The number the review layer exists to deliver, and why you do not have it

A week of review produces two counts: how many items were flagged, and how many of those turned out to be genuinely wrong. Three quantities sit behind them — the model’s error rate, the catch rate, the false-alarm rate — so the observation is a curve through those three, not a point on it. Every row below reproduces the same two counts exactly.

Planted items are what close it: they are the only items whose true label is known before the reviewer sees them, so the catch rate on them is an estimate with a denominator. The interval is exact — a binomial tail inverted, not a normal approximation to a count of a few dozen.

Sensitivity known only to a range

Nobody knows a reviewer’s sensitivity to three decimals. The catch rate a range delivers is then an integral of the law over that range, not the law evaluated at the middle of it, and the two differ in a direction that is not an accident.

Checked against items drawn one at a time

Every headline above is closed form. These two simulations exist so the closed forms can be wrong in public: one draws reviewers’ evidence for a fixed pool of right and wrong items, the other runs a whole stream through routing, planting and review and counts what slipped.

The control

What is actually being measured

Every quantity on this page comes out of three declared objects and nothing else.

  • An item is a coin. The model got it right or it did not, with probability e. There is no text, no task, no difficulty — nothing that a language model does appears anywhere in this engine, because none of it is needed to show the mechanism.
  • A reviewer is a detector. An item the model got wrong presents evidence drawn from a unit normal centred at d′; an item it got right, from a unit normal centred at zero. They raise a flag when the evidence clears a threshold k. d′ is skill — how far apart the two piles are — and k is policy. A single “reviewer accuracy” number is a reading of both at once, which is why it moves when neither of them has.
  • A cost is one number: how many false alarms one missed error is worth. It is the only thing that decides where the threshold should sit, and in almost every real review process it has never been written down. The reviewer is then choosing it, item by item, from whatever the last hundred items felt like.

Why the threshold moves

Expected cost per item is e · ratio · (1 − catch) + (1 − e) · falseAlarm. Differentiate with respect to the threshold, set it to zero, and the two normal densities cancel into a single condition: the likelihood ratio at the threshold equals the odds against an error divided by the price of a miss. Taking logs turns that into the closed form the page uses.

Nothing in that derivation is about attention, fatigue or complacency. A reviewer who is perfectly attentive and perfectly calibrated and doing arithmetic in their head lands in exactly the same place. Real reviewers also get tired, and that would push the same way — but it is not needed, and leaving it out is what makes the result hard to argue with.

The consequence is the whole page. The threshold is a function of the base rate. The base rate is a function of the model. So the catch rate, the false-alarm rate, the size of the queue and the fraction of the queue that turns out to be a real error are all functions of the model being reviewed — and every one of them moves when the model ships, with nobody having changed a policy.

What went against the build

What this does not model

  • Fatigue, distraction, time pressure. All of them exist and all of them push the catch rate the same way. The page leaves them out on purpose: the result is stronger from a reviewer who is behaving optimally than from one who is behaving badly.
  • Unequal variances. Real detection tasks rarely have the two piles equally spread, and an unequal-variance model bends the ROC. The direction of the threshold movement does not depend on that; the exact numbers do.
  • A reviewer who is wrong about the base rate. Here they know it. A reviewer who over-estimates how often the model errs will over-flag, and one who under-estimates will under-flag — both are the same mechanism with a different input, and the planted-item design works by moving that input on purpose.
  • Correlated items. Errors arrive in clusters in real systems, which makes every count on this page more variable than a binomial. It does not move any of the means.
  • What happens after a catch. A flagged error is assumed fixed. If the repair is itself unreliable, every catch rate here is an upper bound on what the customer sees.
  • The cost model itself. One number for the price of a miss is a simplification of a distribution: some misses are embarrassing and some end up in a regulator’s inbox. Sweeping that number is the whole second table for exactly that reason.
