Step 1 — A streaming answer has two clocks, and only one of them is a wait

When you send a prompt to a model that streams, nothing appears for a while, and then words start arriving. The silent part is time to first token, TTFT: everything that happens between you pressing enter and the first character landing on your screen. During that window the user has no evidence that anything is happening at all, which is why it feels longer than it is. It is the only part of the request where the interface has nothing to show. Treat it as a wait, and budget it the way you would budget a page load.

Step 2 — The second clock is a cadence, not a wait

After the first token the request is no longer waiting, it is playing. Time per output token, TPOT, is the gap between consecutive tokens, and what the user perceives is a reading speed. An average adult reads prose at roughly 250 words a minute, which is about five or six tokens a second, so a TPOT under about 180 ms already outruns the reader. Pushing TPOT below that buys you nothing a human can detect, while pushing it above it makes the text visibly stutter. TPOT is a threshold to stay under, not a number to minimise.

Step 3 — They compose into the number people quote, exactly

End-to-end latency is not a third measurement, it is these two glued together: e2e = TTFT + TPOT × (output tokens − 1). The minus one is there because the first token is already paid for inside TTFT. This identity holds per request, exactly, and the simulator asserts it to floating-point tolerance for all 300 of them. It is worth writing down because it tells you where a total went: a slow total is either a long silence or a slow cadence or a long answer, and those have three different fixes. A total on its own cannot tell you which.

Step 4 — A mean is the wrong summary for a streaming interface

Averaging latency assumes the thing you are averaging is roughly symmetric, and queueing latency never is. A queue produces a distribution with a long right tail, so the mean sits above most requests and below the worst ones, describing neither. Worse, a mean over end-to-end latency is dominated by how many tokens the answer had, which is a property of the question, not of your server. Two deploys can have identical mean latency while one of them makes a tenth of users stare at an empty box for several seconds. Report TTFT and TPOT separately, each at p50, p90 and p99, and the empty box becomes visible.

Step 5 — TTFT is prefill plus queueing, and queueing wins

TTFT has two ingredients. The first is prefill: the model has to read the whole prompt before it can write anything, and that cost scales with prompt tokens, so a 4,000-token prompt starts slower than a 200-token one no matter how idle the machine is. The second is the queue: if every slot is busy, your request waits for one to free before its prefill even starts. Queueing is the larger term the moment utilisation gets close to capacity, and it grows without bound while prefill grows linearly. That is why TTFT is the metric that explodes under load and the one worth alerting on.

Step 6 — TPOT is batch size and memory bandwidth, not prompt length

A decode step reads the model's weights out of memory once and uses them for every sequence in the batch. The fixed part of a step is therefore a memory-bandwidth cost that does not care how many sequences there are, and the variable part is small. Run one sequence alone and it pays the whole fixed cost itself; run thirty-two and they share it, which is why throughput climbs with the batch. But each individual sequence now waits for a longer step, so TPOT rises with batch size even though per-token cost falls. Prompt length does not enter this at all: once prefill is done, a long prompt and a short one decode at the same cadence.

Step 7 — Tuning for throughput moves the cost onto someone's TTFT

Throughput is the server's metric and latency is the user's, and the batch is the dial that trades one for the other. Raising it packs more sequences into every weight read, so tokens per second climbs steeply at first and then flattens as the variable term takes over. The bill arrives in two places: every sequence's cadence slows, and — if the scheduler waits to accumulate a full batch before starting — every arriving request sits in that waiting window before its prefill begins. The second effect is pure TTFT, paid by the user, invisible on a throughput dashboard. A capacity plan that only reports tokens per second will keep recommending a bigger batch forever.

Step 8 — The clean separation leaks: prefill interferes with decode

Step 6 said prompt length does not touch TPOT, and in isolation that is exactly true. On a loaded server it is false, and the reason is scheduling rather than arithmetic. A prefill occupies the accelerator, so while one request is reading its 4,000-token prompt, every sequence already streaming is not getting a token. Their cadence stutters, and the stutter scales with other people's prompt lengths. This is the effect chunked prefill exists to spread out, and it is the reason a TPOT regression can be caused by a change nobody made to decoding.

Step 9 — A percentile over requests is not a percentile over tokens

When you compute p99 TPOT you usually average each request's cadence into one number and take the percentile over those numbers. That gives every request one vote, so a two-token reply counts exactly as much as a two-thousand-token one. But a user does not experience requests, they experience tokens, and the long answers contribute nearly all of them. Weight each request's TPOT by how many tokens it actually emitted and the percentile moves, sometimes a lot. Neither number is wrong; they answer different questions, and a dashboard that does not say which one it is answering is not reporting anything.

Step 10 — Budget the two separately, because one SLO cannot hold both

A latency budget for a streaming product is two targets, not one: a TTFT ceiling that keeps the empty box short, and a TPOT ceiling that keeps the text ahead of the reader. They are bounded by different resources and they fail under different conditions, so a single end-to-end SLO will be met on average while one of them is broken for a tenth of traffic. Write them down at p90 or p99, never at the mean, and state the output length you assumed when you converted them into an end-to-end figure. Then when the total moves, the identity in Step 3 tells you which of the three terms moved. That is the entire value of splitting the number.
